## Breaking Down Your Monthly Payment (PITI)

When people talk about a "mortgage payment," they are usually referring to a combination of four distinct expenses, commonly known in the real estate industry as **PITI** (Principal, Interest, Taxes, and Insurance).

### 1. Principal
The principal is the actual amount of money you borrowed from the bank to buy the house. If you buy a $400,000 home and put $80,000 down, your starting principal balance is $320,000. In the early years of a 30-year mortgage, only a very small fraction of your monthly payment actually goes toward reducing this principal balance.

### 2. Interest
Interest is the fee the lender charges you for the privilege of borrowing their money. It is calculated as a percentage of your outstanding principal balance. Because your balance is highest at the very beginning of the loan, the majority of your early monthly payments go straight to paying interest, not paying down the house.

### 3. Taxes (Property Tax)
Local governments assess property taxes to fund public services like schools, roads, and emergency responders. This tax is calculated based on the assessed value of your home. Lenders typically collect 1/12th of your annual property tax bill every month and hold it in an escrow account, paying the government on your behalf when the bill is due.

### 4. Insurance (Homeowners & PMI)
*   **Homeowners Insurance**: Protects your property against disasters, fire, and theft. Like taxes, lenders usually collect this monthly and hold it in escrow.
*   **Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)**: If you put down less than 20% of the home's purchase price, conventional lenders will legally require you to pay for PMI. This insurance protects the *lender* (not you) in case you default on the loan. PMI can add hundreds of dollars to your monthly payment and is a crucial expense to calculate.

## How the Math Works (The Amortization Formula)

If you are curious about the mechanics behind the calculator, mortgages use a standard amortized loan formula to calculate the Equated Monthly Installment (EMI) for the Principal and Interest portion of your payment.

The complex mathematical formula is:
$$ M = P \frac{i(1+i)^n}{(1+i)^n - 1} $$

Where:
*   **M:** Total monthly payment (Principal + Interest only)
*   **P:** Principal loan amount (Purchase price minus down payment)
*   **i:** Monthly interest rate (Annual interest rate divided by 12)
*   **n:** Total number of payments (Months)

Our tool performs this calculation instantly and generates a full **Amortization Schedule**—a vital table that shows you exactly how much of your payment goes to interest versus principal for every single month of the 30-year loan.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What is the difference between a 15-year and a 30-year mortgage?
A 30-year mortgage is the industry standard because it stretches the loan out over three decades, resulting in the lowest possible monthly payment. However, because you are borrowing the money for twice as long, you will pay staggeringly more in total interest. 

A 15-year mortgage comes with substantially higher monthly payments, but you will own the home free and clear in half the time, and you will save tens or even hundreds of thousands of dollars in interest charges. Furthermore, lenders reward 15-year borrowers with lower interest rates. 

### How much should I put down on a house?
The traditional "gold standard" is a **20% down payment**. Putting 20% down immediately gives you substantial equity in the property and entirely eliminates the need for expensive Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI). However, there are many first-time homebuyer programs (like FHA loans) that allow down payments as low as 3.5%, though you will pay strict mortgage insurance premiums.

### Does a lower interest rate really matter that much?
Yes. On a 30-year mortgage for $350,000, the difference between a 6.0% interest rate and a 7.0% interest rate is roughly $230 *every single month*. Over the life of the 30-year loan, that 1% difference will cost you over **$80,000** in extra interest payments. This is why having an excellent credit score before applying for a mortgage is fiercely important.
