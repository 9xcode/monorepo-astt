## The Rise of QR Codes

Quick Response (QR) codes have transitioned from niche logistics trackers to universal interfaces. Today, they are printed on restaurant tables, product packaging, utility bills, and digital advertisements. The ease of scanning a code using a mobile phone camera has made physical-to-digital interactions frictionless.

However, this convenience comes with hidden security risks. Because QR codes are designed for machines rather than humans, you cannot visually determine if a code leads to a legitimate website or a malicious URL.

---

## Understanding QR Code Security Risks

A QR code is simply a visual representation of data — usually a web link. When you scan it, your device automatically translates the black-and-white grid pattern into text or a URL and prompts you to open it. Attackers exploit this automatic translation in several ways:

### 1. "Quishing" (QR Code Phishing)
Similar to traditional phishing emails, quishing involves placing malicious QR codes in public places, on flyer printouts, or even in emails. When scanned, these codes direct you to fake login portals designed to steal your bank credentials, social media passwords, or personal details.

### 2. Malicious App Downloads
Attackers can encode links that trigger automatic file downloads (such as `.apk` files on Android) that install spyware, keyloggers, or adware on your phone.

### 3. Contact or Wi-Fi Manipulation
QR codes can do more than open websites. They can trigger actions like:
- Automatically drafting and sending a text message to a premium rate number.
- Connecting your phone to an unsecured, rogue Wi-Fi hotspot.
- Adding a contact containing malicious links to your address book.

## Best Practices for Safe Scanning

To protect your personal data, adopt these security habits whenever you scan a QR code:

### 1. Preview the Decoded Link
Never use a scanner that automatically opens links without showing you the URL first. Always inspect the destination URL:
- Check for misspellings (e.g., `paypal-login.com` instead of `paypal.com`).
- Beware of shortened URLs (like `bit.ly` or `tinyurl.com`) in public places, as they conceal the final destination.

### 2. Inspect the Physical Context
Before scanning a QR code on a restaurant table, parking meter, or public flyer, feel the surface. Attackers frequently print malicious QR code stickers and paste them directly on top of legitimate, original codes. If the sticker feels raised or misaligned, do not scan it.

### 3. Use Secure, Privacy-First Tools
Avoid downloading scanner apps that request excessive permissions (such as access to your contacts, location, or file storage). Use a client-side scanning tool that processes images completely inside your browser and lets you copy or preview the link safely before opening it.

### 4. Double-Check Requests for Personal Information
If a QR code scanned at a restaurant or parking lot directs you to a portal that demands credit card details, phone numbers, or passwords, proceed with caution. Legitimate establishments rarely require you to log in or input payment credentials just to view a menu.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a QR code hack my phone just by scanning it?
Simply scanning a code to read its text cannot hack your phone. The danger lies in what you do *after* scanning: clicking a malicious link, downloading an unverified file, or inputting your credentials on a fake website.

### What should I do if I scanned a suspicious code?
If you scanned a code and suspect it was malicious:
- **Do not** click any links or download any files.
- If you were directed to a website and entered any passwords, change those passwords immediately on the official website.
- Scan your device with an updated security tool if you downloaded any files.
